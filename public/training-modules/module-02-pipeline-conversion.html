The client mentioned the same need twice. Nobody wrote it down as an opportunity.

In two separate calls, three months apart, Solera's Finance Manager mentioned that Solera was "looking at a new supplier finance arrangement" for their import lines. Efua noted it both times — as colour for her call memo, not as something to size and log. By the third call, Solera already had the facility. With another bank.

Nothing about the calls was mishandled. The need was heard, both times. It just never became a sized, owned entry — so nothing forced anyone to act on it before someone else did.

THE BEHAVIOUR

Every time a client states a need — a number, a date, a plan — convert it into a pipeline entry before you leave the call: sized, probability-banded, with an owner and a date.

That's the whole module. Everything from here on is how to do exactly that, using a real call as the walk-through.

Why this matters, in one line: a signal you don't log is a signal you're trusting yourself to remember — and a competitor to forget.

This follows on from wallet-share mapping: once you know where the gaps are, this is how you catch the moment one of them starts to move — instead of finding out after it's closed.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Solera Foods Ghana Ltd — a routine call

The call, in brief. Efua's monthly catch-up with Linda Owusu-Ansah, Solera's Ghana Finance Manager. Nothing unusual on the agenda. Fifteen minutes in, Linda mentions that Solera Group AG has approved a new processing line for the Ghana plant, expected to roughly double output within the next year — equipment will need to be imported. She also mentions, almost as an aside, that Solera's existing import facility for spare parts, held at another bank, comes up for renewal in the first quarter.

Two sentences, said in passing. Neither sounded like a pitch. That's exactly why they're easy to let pass as background.

Step 1 — recognise the signal. Efua doesn't wait to see if Linda repeats herself. She writes down both statements the moment the call ends — dated, attributed, specific — instead of trusting herself to remember the detail by Friday.

Capacity-doubling capex, equipment to be imported
LOG NOW
Competitor-held facility, renewal date already known
WATCH

Step 2 — match the need to a product. Equipment being imported and paid for in foreign currency is a trade-finance and FX conversation — an import facility tied to the capex plan, not a vague "maybe some lending." Efua writes the match down specifically, in words Access actually sells.

Step 3 — size it, conceptually. No exact figure has been shared yet. Sizing is a way of thinking — volume × spread × probability — not a formula to memorise. Efua works from what she has: the scale of Solera's current facility, the "roughly doubling" comment, and typical costs for a comparable processing line, to build an illustrative range — say, GHS 40m–60m in equipment import value. She marks it clearly as an estimate to refine once real numbers exist, not a final figure.

Step 4 — set a probability band, then an owner and a date. Nothing is approved. No budget is confirmed, and Efua has never met Kojo Antwi-Danso, the Group CFO who actually holds the capex decision. That puts this at roughly 25% — a real, live signal, but early. She logs the entry with an owner (herself) and a next action with a date: confirm the capex timeline and budget range with Kojo within two weeks, using Linda for the introduction. The entry goes into the pipeline the same day — not after the memo is typed up, not "when she gets to it."

A pipeline entry without an owner and a date isn't an entry — it's a note to self, and notes to self get lost.
