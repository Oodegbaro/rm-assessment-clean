HOOK

A week of quick replies to the wrong client.

By Friday, Efua realised most of her week had gone to Kessben Logistics Gh Ltd — quick replies to small requests that landed easily by phone and WhatsApp. Meanwhile, Solera Foods Ghana Ltd's finance manager, Linda Owusu-Ansah, had sent a time-sensitive request on Monday about an upcoming facility renewal. It sat unanswered until Thursday, simply because it needed more digging and hadn't arrived with three follow-up calls.

Efua hadn't ignored Solera. Kessben was just easier to answer first — and a full week of "easier first" is how a higher-value client ends up waiting the longest.

THE BEHAVIOUR

Before you commit time this week, check the tier list — and defer the lower-tier ask, gracefully, when it competes with a higher-tier client's need.

That's the whole module. Efua covers several accounts, not just Solera — Kessben Logistics and Ayeyie Textiles among them — and they aren't all worth the same amount of her time. Someone already did the work of ranking them into tiers as part of the account-planning she covered earlier in this series. The gap isn't that the tier list doesn't exist; it's that it doesn't get consulted in the moment two requests actually compete for the same afternoon. Everything from here is how she closes that gap, and how she says "not yet" to one client without damaging the relationship.

Why this matters, in one line: tiering exists to protect scarce time for the accounts where it matters most — it isn't paperwork for its own sake.

The habit itself is small: a fast tier check before you commit an afternoon, not a research project.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Two Tuesday requests, one afternoon

The situation. Tuesday morning, two things land close together. Kessben's operations contact sends a WhatsApp asking for a quick letter confirming account status for a routine audit — easy, maybe ten minutes of work. Separately, Linda from Solera has a request that's been open since Monday: a working-capital top-up ahead of Solera's peak seasonal stock buy. It needs a proper credit write-up, not a quick reply.

The tier check. Efua opens her account-tier list from the earlier account-planning work. It takes about thirty seconds — she isn't re-researching either account, just confirming what she already knows: Solera is Tier 1, her highest-value relationship with real growth potential. Kessben is Tier 3, a smaller, stable book with limited near-term upside.

Convenience isn't value. Kessben's ask feels urgent because it's easy — one call, one signed letter, done fast. Solera's ask feels harder because it takes real work. Efua reminds herself: how easy something is to answer has nothing to do with how much it's worth to Access. Left unanswered much longer, Solera's request risks the client sourcing the top-up elsewhere, right before a season that matters commercially.

Deferring Kessben, gracefully. Efua doesn't ignore Kessben. She calls the operations contact, gives a clear day — "I can have this letter to you by Thursday" — and checks whether the audit deadline is actually firmer than that. She doesn't need to name Solera; she just says a time-sensitive matter on another account has to come first this week.

What actually happens. Efua turns to Linda's request first. By Friday, Solera has an answer it can act on before peak season, and Kessben has its routine letter delivered on the day promised — not urgently, but on schedule and without any bruised feelings.

Tier tells you where the value is. Urgency tells you what's shouting loudest right now. Check both before you decide which one wins the afternoon.

If you need to defer, say this

"I want to get this right for you — I can have this back to you by [day]. If anything about the timing changes on your end, tell me and I'll take another look."

Escalate instead of deferring when

  • A lower-tier account has an unusually time-sensitive, high-value moment — for example, a one-off large tender or an opportunity that won't wait.
  • You're genuinely unsure which request should come first — raise it rather than guess.

The tier check and the deferral script are two separate habits. Use the check to decide who goes first; use the script to make sure the client who waits still feels looked after, not dropped.
