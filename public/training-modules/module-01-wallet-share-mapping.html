HOOK

You've covered this client for two years. How much of their business do you actually hold?

Efua has covered Solera Foods Ghana Ltd for two years. The Finance Manager always answers her calls. No complaints have ever come up in a review. It feels like a strong relationship.

When Efua actually mapped it against every product Solera could plausibly be buying from a bank, Access held one thing: a current account. Nothing about the relationship was wrong. The gap was simply invisible — because no one had ever laid it out.

This is the single most common reason a "good" account underdelivers: not a bad relationship, a missing map.

THE BEHAVIOUR

Before your next client review, map what Access holds against the client's whole banking relationship — then pick one gap worth chasing first.

That's the whole module. Everything from here on is how to do exactly that, using a real account as the walk-through.

Why this matters, in one line: most of the corporate wallet that's unclaimed isn't sitting with a stranger — it's sitting with a client Access already banks, just not for very much.

This is the "Think" step of account planning — building the fact base before you plan or engage. The rest of the account-planning discipline builds on this map.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Solera Foods Ghana Ltd

The account, in brief. Solera Foods Ghana Ltd is a packaged food and beverage manufacturer — subsidiary of a foreign parent group, about 60 independent distributors nationwide, estimated FY revenue GHS 2.1bn. Access holds their day-to-day current account and handles some local transfers. That's it.

Efua's first move isn't to guess what's missing. It's to write down, plainly, everything Access already holds — no product category skipped, no "we probably do some of that."

Step 1 — list what Access actually holds. Current account: yes. Local transfers: yes, occasionally. Lending: no. FX: no. Trade finance: no. Salary/payroll mandate: no. That's the whole list — one product, out of at least five plausible categories.

Step 2 — estimate the total size, without insider numbers. Efua doesn't have Solera's internal banking figures, and it's too early in the relationship to ask for them directly. So she uses a working shortcut instead of a guess: roughly 3% of a client's turnover is a common proxy for their total banking revenue pool (or, if she can estimate it, their actual interest expense plus about 10% of their free cash float).

For Solera: 3% of an estimated GHS 2.1bn turnover ≈ GHS 63m estimated total banking revenue pool, spread across however many banks Solera uses.

Step 3 — work out Access's actual share. From the one product Access holds, Efua estimates Access's own revenue from Solera at roughly GHS 2.5m a year. Against the GHS 63m estimate: 2.5 ÷ 63 ≈ 4%. That's the number from the hook — now Efua has it in writing, with a method behind it, not a feeling.

A number without a method can't be defended in a review. This one can.

Step 4 — prioritise, don't chase everything. Efua has four gaps: lending, FX, trade finance, salary mandate. The salary mandate is the largest number on paper. But she's never met Solera's Treasury team or HR decision-makers — she'd be opening a new relationship and pitching a major mandate switch at the same time.

Trade finance is smaller in headline size, but Efua already has warm contact with several of Solera's distributors through the Finance Manager she knows well. She picks distributor trade finance to pursue first — not because it's the biggest gap, but because it's the one she can actually reach right now.

The right first gap is the one where size and access line up — not just the biggest number on the page.
